## KQ1. What do developers of Goat Simulator, Untitled Goose Game, Gang Beasts, Human Fall Flat, Fall Guys, Moving Out, Chained Together (plus Overcooked / Pico Park) say about designing comedy? What specific techniques?

### Takeaway
Across every studio the recipe is the same four parts: (1) give the player a body that fails in readable, harmless ways (floppy physics, stubby limbs, "nasty bugs" kept on purpose); (2) build a world of rule-followers who *react* visibly to being disturbed (reaction animations, annoyed faces, chasing); (3) make conflict legible and telegraphed so the laugh comes from anticipation plus a surprise payoff; (4) make failure cheap to retry. Developers repeatedly say the comedy was found by iteration and playtesting, not written up front, and that "nobody actually gets hurt" is the line that keeps slapstick funny instead of cruel.

**Untitled Goose Game (House House)**
- Nico Disseldorp: "we just kept trying to think of funny things for a goose to do, and funny ways for people to react, until eventually a game started to solidify around the idea." - Disseldorp calls it "half stealth simulator, half silent comedy film" and a "slapstick immersive sim"; on their earlier game Push Me Pull You: "the humour comes from the uncanny movements of these strange bodies, and from the strange communication required to do anything when you and your partner are joined together." - The game sold "over 100,000 copies in its first two weeks" and began as an "in-joke" from a stock photo of a goose posted in the team chat — [It's Nice That, 25 Nov 2019](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/house-house-untitled-goose-game-digital-251119)

### Inferences
- The techniques these studios actually name are: imperfect physics kept on purpose; characters built *from* the physics (short legs, floppy arms); NPC reaction systems (idle → disturbed → chase); telegraphed set-ups (a to-do list, a visible hazard) so the player anticipates; luck that "mitigates skill" so everyone fails sometimes; punctuating sound/music; short, legible scenes; taunt/hug mechanics to support non-violent play. Nobody describes writing jokes — the humor is systemic and emergent.
- For a horse game the direct analogues are: a horse body that can refuse/stumble/ragdoll in readable ways; a show-ground full of serious rule-followers (judges, stewards, ponies in the warm-up) who react; a course that is "one sentence to explain"; and the ACMI "nobody actually gets hurt" rule as a hard design constraint (falls must be cartoon falls with an instant, funny remount).
- Fall Guys' "any advantage from skill is mitigated" is the pattern most likely to keep a less-skilled younger sibling laughing instead of losing.

## KQ2. What do reviews and kid/parent voices say makes these games hilarious with friends and siblings — and which interactions cause fights?

### Takeaway
Kids and parents consistently name "chaos you caused together" as the laugh, and "being targeted / someone sabotaging on purpose / one sibling rage-quitting" as the fight. Reviewers of Pico Park, Overcooked, Gang Beasts and Chained Together use the same formula — "stressful in the moment, hilarious in retrospect" — which points to the fail-reel/replay as a design lever.

### Cited Findings
- Fall Guys, Common Sense Media (age 8+; 68 kid reviews): kids "express frustration with certain gameplay mechanics, particularly when it comes to being targeted by others or experiencing lag"; parents (15 reviews) say it is "fun for all ages" but "some express a need for parental guidance due to the competitive elements and potential frustrations it can cause". Violence note: "Players can bump each other around the obstacle course, even knock them off" — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/fall-guys-ultimate-knockout)
- Fall Guys griefing: griefers "stand by the finishing line without crossing it"; advice is "repeatedly jump to struggle your way free" and "try to accept them as one of this fun BR game's humorous quirks… try not to take it personally" — [GameRevolution, 10 Aug 2020](https://www.gamerevolution.com/guides/655429-how-to-stop-fall-guys-griefers-from-grabbing-you)
- Untitled Goose Game, Common Sense Media (age 8+; kids say 6+): "The game encourages bad behavior, including stealing items from people, causing accidents, and otherwise ruining people's day"; "it's more about being a nuisance than being a threat" — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/untitled-goose-game)

### Inferences
- The fight triggers are consistent: (1) *being singled out* by another player (Fall Guys "targeted by others"), (2) *asymmetric tools* (paid punch/kick emotes), (3) *one person deliberately sabotaging* (Pico Park), (4) *losing something you built* (Steal a Brainrot "kids crying"), (5) *perceived unfairness* between siblings of different skill (Mario Kart blue shell, Smash "accidental" knock-off).
- The laugh triggers are: shared chaos with "janky" physics, watching a friend's slapstick failure, and the retrospective replay. The laugh and the fight share the same mechanics — the difference is whether the victim loses something that matters.
- Design lever confirmed by three independent reviewers' phrasing ("hilarious in retrospect"): an automatic end-of-round fail-reel converts in-the-moment rage into the shared laugh.

## KQ3. What do tweens (10–14) find funny according to humor-development research and kids' media reports?

### Takeaway
Research is thin for 10–14 specifically, but what exists points the same way: by 10–14 the audience is well past "babyish" riddles and into (a) visual/action slapstick, (b) aggressive-but-harmless humor (someone getting their comeuppance), (c) irony and knowing "it's junk" meme humor, and (d) humor as social currency with friends. Gaming itself is now "how they hang out with friends" for almost every 8–12-year-old.

### Cited Findings
- Content analysis of 107 TV series (5,633 scenes) that 10-to-14-year-olds named as their favourites found ten humor types — "aggressive, slapstick, self-defeating, sexual, irreverent, coping, parody, wordplay, incongruity, absurdity" — "with aggressive humor most often and coping humor least often present (in 87.9% versus 23.4% of series, respectively). Humor types also frequently co-occurred." (van der Wal, Piotrowski, Fikkers, Valkenburg, 2020) — [Utrecht University research portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/more-than-just-a-laughing-matter-a-coding-framework-of-humor-in-m/)
- Paired interviews with 8–11-year-olds (Keele, 2016): "use of both self-enhancing and self-defeating humor were apparent in some children, but not all", i.e. the four adult humor styles (affiliative, self-enhancing, aggressive, self-defeating) are already partly in place by 11 — [Europe's Journal of Psychology](https://ejop.psychopen.eu/index.php/ejop/article/view/1067)
- Paul McGhee's stage model summarised for children's writers: "Children in early elementary grades love broad physical humor, such as pie in the face, pants falling down to expose underwear, slipping on a banana peel"; "It's especially funny if a 'serious' character (such as a parent or teacher) unwittingly does these things"; "From about second grade on, kids begin to appreciate verbal humor (witty banter, sarcasm, irony, and so on in dialogue) and have the patience to allow a joke to be set up over several scenes." — [Dummies: What Kids Consider Funny](https://dummies.com/article/what-kids-consider-funny-169343)
- McGhee on why older kids groan: riddles "are most funny in 1st and 2nd grade, but become progressively less funny after that, because they are just too easy to understand" — (search snippet) [LaughterRemedy](https://www.laughterremedy.com/?page_id=32)
- A Turkish study applying McGhee's stages to 2–13-year-olds found the 5th stage in "the group of children, from 11-year-old to 13-year-old" and that "children reacted by laughing most to the examples including visual and action scenes and they reacted by laughing least to the examples including verbal statements" — (search snippet; PDF not fetched) [YÖK thesis repository](https://acikbilim.yok.gov.tr/handle/20.500.12812/40424)
- McGhee's own book covers development only "from infancy until age seven or eight years" — there is no McGhee stage written specifically for 10–14 — [LaughterRemedy book page](https://www.laughterremedy.com/?page_id=32)
- Kids' media: YPulse "Tweens: Gaming" (30 Jun 2026): "Almost every tween plays video games—and most are playing every single day. For Gen Alpha, gaming isn't just a hobby; it's how they hang out with friends, how they discover brands, and a core part of how they define themselves. Roblox, Minecraft, and Fortnite are the virtual spaces where tween friendships live" — [YPulse](https://www.ypulse.com/report/2026/06/30/tweens-gaming/) (search snippet adds: survey of 1,500 US/Canadian 8–12s in April 2026)
- Brainrot humor is knowingly ironic: "The word started as slang for low-quality, hyper-stimulating internet content, the kind of stuff that's funny for ten seconds and forgotten in eleven. Kids use it knowingly, and a little ironically. They know it's junk. That's part of the joke." Brainrot games are "fast, silly, hyper-viral Roblox experiences built around internet meme culture"; a brainrot is an "AI-generated creature[] with nonsense names and absurd designs" — [Kinzoo parent guide, 5 Jun 2026](https://www.kinzoo.com/blog/what-are-brainrot-games-a-parents-guide-to-the-roblox-trend)
- Gen Alpha slang in play: "If you've heard your kid mention '6-7' or call something 'Ohio' while playing, you're witnessing the frontline of Gen Alpha culture… Calling a map 'Ohio' is just their way of saying it's weird, cursed, or generally 'cringe.'" "For a 10-year-old, being 'in the lobby' with friends is the modern equivalent of hanging out at the park." — [Screenwise Fortnite guide, updated 13 Oct 2026](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/fortnite-and-battle-royale-culture)
- Kids in their own words (ages 9–11, 2018): "My favorite parts of Fortnite are communicating with kids and getting high kills" (Max, 11); "Being able to talk to friends is really exciting. It's like a virtual playground" (Nell); "Each battle is different and created in a random way, so you never know what you're going to have to deal with" (Junior) — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/pop/104193/why-children-love-the-fortnite-video-game-so-much-in-their-own-words)
- Ofcom 2025: "approximately 89% of children aged 3–17 play games on at least one device" (search snippet; Ofcom pages returned 403) — [Computing at School summary](https://www.computingatschool.org.uk/forum-news-blogs/2025/may/understanding-media-use-among-children-aged-3-11-key-insights-from-ofcom-s-2025-media-literacy-report/)

### Inferences
- "Babyish" for a 10–11-year-old = pure knock-knock/riddle humor and cute-for-its-own-sake; "funny" = someone (ideally a serious authority figure or a friend) visibly failing, irony they're in on, and absurd creatures with ridiculous names. This matches exactly what the developer evidence in KQ1 produces (slapstick + reacting rule-followers) and what Roblox hits exploit (absurd names, stealing).
- Because the favourite-series analysis found aggressive humor in 88% of what 10–14s choose, "mean" cannot be designed out entirely — it has to be converted into *harmless* aggression (the ACMI slapstick rule) where the target bounces back instantly.
- Verbal gags should be a garnish, not the main course: both the "it's not puns" GDC framing and the visual > verbal laughter finding point there, and a phone/iPad co-op game can't rely on reading mid-jump anyway.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that directly measures what 10–14-year-olds laugh at *in games*; the van der Wal paper is about TV content, not reactions.
- No Dubit, Kids Industries or The Insights Family report on humor was retrievable; Roblox's own "what tweens like" research was not found.
- YPulse's full findings on emotes/humor are paywalled; only the summary was readable.

## KQ4. How do co-op games let players mess with each other without griefing (bumping, throwing, stealing, swapping, sabotage), and how do designers limit it?

### Takeaway
The strongest piece of evidence is a documented case (A Virus Named Tom): playtesters hated bumping into each other until the collision got a sound, a bounce and an annoyed, cursing victim face — then they "actually laughed as they annoyed each other", and the designer *rewarded* bouncing teammates into harm. The limiters that recur across games are: keep sabotage cheap-to-recover-from, make it a gamble for the saboteur, pool the score, give everyone the same tools, add assist modes/handicaps for the weaker sibling, and keep communication to pre-made emotes.

### Cited Findings
- A Virus Named Tom (Tim Keenan, 2012): playtesters "had become frustrated by their characters colliding into each other… They almost unanimously suggested allowing players to pass through one another, but Keenan likened that idea to turning off friendly fire in a first-person shooter". Collision "forces players to communicate… and it actually enabled another appealing part of the game: griefing, or players having fun screwing each other over." Fix: "We now have a sound, the TOMs bounce off each other, and the TOM that was hit looks really annoyed and curses (in garbled virus-speak) at the other TOM. This addition alone made collisions more enjoyable, and encouraged that sort of griefing." Result: "playtesters were less bothered when bumping into each other, and actually laughed as they annoyed each other. The developer emphasized griefing even more by adding a reward for bouncing teammates into harm's way." — [Game Developer, 24 Aug 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/sometimes-frustrating-design-can-be-fun)
- Gang Beasts deliberately supports non-violent interaction (hug, hand-holding) alongside taunts; taunting exists because "a big part of the fun is coming back after your opponent thinks he's been victorious" — [bit-tech, 2014](https://bit-tech.net/reviews/gaming/pc/interview-gang-beasts/1/)
- Fall Guys grabbing: a self-identified lead designer explained on Reddit (2020) that a grab ends either when the grabber lets go or a grab "break" applies a force to both Fall Guys, and "because it's a physics based game this 'force' can be a bit unpredictable" — (search snippet; Reddit inaccessible, secondhand) [r/FallGuysGame](https://www.reddit.com/r/FallGuysGame/comments/i5mqpb/im_sorry/)
- Fall Guys griefing mitigation in practice is recovery speed: "Getting back into a match doesn't take long and you won't have lost too much progress" — [GameRevolution](https://www.gamerevolution.com/guides/655429-how-to-stop-fall-guys-griefers-from-grabbing-you)
- Overcooked's throwing was added to fix idle time, not to attack, and the designers "ended up having to add restrictions to stop players feeling frustrated" when too many mistakes were possible — [Wikipedia: Overcooked 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overcooked_2); [Game Developer IGF interview](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-ghost-town-games-i-overcooked-i-)
- Moving Out and Overcooked: All You Can Eat ship an Assist Mode that lets you "toggle various elements of the game to suit your needs" / "slows down timers" — [WellPlayed](https://www.well-played.com.au/made-in-australia-we-talk-moving-out-with-smg-studio/); [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/couch-co-op-games-that-bring-families-together)
- Mario Party Jamboree: per-player "star handicaps" to even out older vs younger players — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/super-mario-party-jamboree)
- Pico Park's rope/push levels: the pushing is the gameplay and the sabotage risk is social ("someone who purposely sabotages the rest of the team"); the frustration is "not from toxic mechanics" — [Daily Campus](https://dailycampus.com/2023/11/14/the-backlog-destroy-your-friendships-with-pico-park/); [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/pico-park-game)
- Chained Together: the shared chain "can get caught on platforms and potentially mess up you and your friends" — the sabotage is symmetrical and physical, and the viral output is fail clips — [Daily Campus](https://dailycampus.com/2024/11/12/the-backlog-ascend-with-friends-in-chained-together/); [Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/subcultures/chained-together)
- Goat Simulator 3 co-op: the saddle lets you "force another player to ride on your back", and it stacks — sabotage that is also a shared ride — [Press Start](https://press-start.com.au/previews/2022/08/27/goat-simulator-3-hands-on-preview-an-even-wilder-sandbox/)
- Steal a Brainrot: the core loop is stealing from other players' bases; peak 25.4M concurrent (Oct 2025), 20M+ in Aug 2025 surpassing Fortnite's 15.3M record — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steal_a_Brainrot); the steal-before-it-reaches-storage mechanic is described by [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/steal-a-brainrot-roblox-empire-review-popular-memes-1851787027). Grow a Garden likewise lets players "steal other users' crops" — [YPulse, 12 Jun 2025](https://www.ypulse.com/newsfeed/2025/06/12/a-teen-allegedly-created-the-viral-grow-a-garden-game-on-roblox-and-young-gamers-are-loving-it/)
- Co-opetition design (Marked for Mayhem, Oct 2026): "Disruptors, abilities that can be thrown at enemies or aimed at fellow Operators to steal points"; "the mission is shared, but the glory isn't"; "Sabotage only works as design if it feels like a gamble rather than a griefing tool, and the line between the two is drawn by players, not by spreadsheets." — [Martin Cid Magazine, 12 Oct 2026](https://www.martincid.com/technology-sv/gaming/marked-for-mayhem-first-public-playtest/)
- Shared vs individual score: Jamestown's developers argue "a single pooled score signals 'you're all in this together,' whereas separate counters push players to compare themselves" — (search snippet; co-optimus page 403) [Co-Optimus](https://co-optimus.com/editorial/976/page/2/indie-ana-co-op-and-the-dev-stories-you-re-all-in-this-together.html)
- Board-game design forum on co-opetition: limit each player's per-turn ability to swing the outcome ("may only have .5% of power to really change the game") so sabotage can't collapse the run — (search snippet; page failed to parse) [BGDF](https://bgdf.com/forum/game-creation/design-theory/oxymoronic-co-opcompetition-game)
- Hellapagos is a self-described "co-opetition" tabletop game for ages 8+, 3–12 players — proof the genre is considered age-appropriate at 8+ — [Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2024/04/17/critical-play-competitive-analysis-hellapagos/)
- Overcooked 2 online: "communication is handled through pre-programmed em[otes]" — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/overcooked-2)

### Inferences
- The A Virus Named Tom case is the clearest template for a horse game: do *not* remove horse-to-horse collisions; instead give every bump a sound, a bounce and a funny annoyed reaction on the victim's horse/rider, and make bumping a teammate into a puddle score a tiny shared bonus so the "victim" is also a performer.
- Observed limiters, consolidated: (1) fast, funny recovery (Fall Guys/HFF "fall, try again"); (2) sabotage as gamble — it costs the prankster something (Marked for Mayhem); (3) per-player power cap/cooldown so no one can collapse the run (BGDF); (4) pooled team score (Jamestown); (5) symmetric tools, nothing bought (Stumble Guys complaint); (6) assist/handicap toggles (Moving Out, Overcooked AYCE, Mario Party); (7) pre-made emotes, no free chat (Overcooked 2, Stumble Guys); (8) support "non-violent" verbs (Gang Beasts hug/hand-hold).
- Stealing (Steal a Brainrot, Grow a Garden) is the single most engaging *and* most tear-inducing mechanic in current tween play; in a sibling co-op it should only ever steal things of zero persistent value (a rosette during the victory lap, a flower off a jump), never progress.

### Gaps
- No primary Mediatonic statement on the grab's design intent or on griefing policy was found; Reddit designer comment is unverifiable.
- No It Takes Two, Mario Kart team-mode or Among Us developer material on friendly sabotage was retrievable within the remaining budget.
- Steal a Brainrot's base-lock/shield timers (its own anti-griefing design) are known from play but I could not fetch a page documenting them; treat as a gap.

## KQ5. Which emote/taunt/reaction systems do tweens use most, and why?

### Takeaway
Emotes are status and in-jokes, not just expression: Fortnite emotes/skins are how tweens "measure social status", Dress to Impress's "Pose 28" became a TikTok meme, and Stumble Guys' punch/kick emotes are popular precisely because they *do something to another player* — which is also why they trigger fairness complaints when sold for money.

### Cited Findings
- Fortnite is "the new digital mall where kids hang out, trade memes, and measure social status through 'skins' and 'emotes.'" — [Screenwise, updated 13 Oct 2026](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/fortnite-and-battle-royale-culture)
- Dress to Impress: "By spring of 2024, Dress to Impress became a meme on TikTok, where users posted comical outfits and about the game's 'Pose 28' emote, which causes avatars to push their pe[lvis]…"; the Charli XCX collaboration added "in-game items, emotes, and patterns" and pushed the game past 651,000 concurrent players — [Wikipedia: Dress to Impress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_to_Impress_(video_game))
- Stumble Guys: "Kick, Punch, hug and banana are all very useful emotes" (player, search snippet) and a thread complaining "punching/kicking emotes are ruining the game… give an unfair advantage to some players" because they are grind- or money-gated — [Steam discussion, Nov 2022](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1677740/discussions/0/3488628040303483921/)
- Gang Beasts added a taunt because the comeback moment is "a big part of the fun", and hug/hand-hold for non-violent play — [bit-tech](https://bit-tech.net/reviews/gaming/pc/interview-gang-beasts/1/)
- Fall Guys: "emotes to show off personality" are flagged by Common Sense as cosmetic shop items — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/fall-guys-ultimate-knockout)
- Overcooked 2 limits online communication to pre-programmed emotes — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/overcooked-2)
- A 2018 PopJam survey found kids on Roblox "love the gameplay, the characters, the dances and the skins" — (search snippet) [SuperAwesome](https://superawesome.com/?p=2912)

### Inferences
- The emotes tweens actually use fall into three jobs: (1) taunt/comeback after a near-loss (Gang Beasts), (2) affect another player physically (Stumble Guys punch/kick/banana, Fall Guys grab), (3) meme-able silly poses for clips (Pose 28). A horse game should ship all three, free, equally for everyone, and make them photogenic for screenshots/clips.
- Horse-specific emote set that maps onto these jobs: horse side-eye, the "nod of absolute agreement", dramatic snort, "spook at own fart", lazy-pony grass-munch; rider shrug, "I meant to do that", crop-drop, victory wave that unbalances the rider.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on which emotes 8–12s use most (YPulse paywalled; Brawl Stars pins and Roblox emote usage not retrievable).

### Inferences

- The horse-game market is a comedy vacuum: the only humor developers mention is unintentional glitches, which they enjoyed. 9. **Silly cosmetics that cause failure, and meme-able poses/emotes.** Evidence: "cumbersome looking costumes… set up for failure" — [PS Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=221489); Pose 28 meme — [Wikipedia DTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_to_Impress_(video_game)); emotes as status — [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/fortnite-and-battle-royale-culture).
10. **Taunt/comeback moments.** Evidence: Gang Beasts taunt exists because "coming back after your opponent thinks he's been victorious" is "a big part of the fun" — [bit-tech](https://bit-tech.net/reviews/gaming/pc/interview-gang-beasts/1/).
11. **Reactive sound/music punctuation.** Evidence: goose piano "stop-starty"; interviewer: it "delay[s] beats in search of the perfect time to add a note of punctuation" — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/behind-the-honk-an-i-untitled-goose-game-i-q-a); TOM collision "sound" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/sometimes-frustrating-design-can-be-fun). Supporting layer, not a standalone mechanic.
12. **Verbal gags, puns, riddles.** Evidence against leading with them for 10–14: "it's not puns" — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1035068/Independent-Games-Summit-Comedy-Through); riddles "progressively less funny" after grade 2 — [LaughterRemedy, snippet](https://www.laughterremedy.com/?page_id=32); laughed least at "verbal statements" — [YÖK, snippet](https://acikbilim.yok.gov.tr/handle/20.500.12812/40424).

### Inferences
- Items 1–5 are the engine; 6–9 are the flavour layer that makes clips shareable; 10–11 are polish; 12 is a garnish. For a phone/iPad browser game, 1–3 also happen to be the mechanics that read at small screen size.
- Mechanic 7 (stealing) ranks high on engagement but must be capped at zero-persistence stakes for siblings (see Deliverable c).

## Deliverable (b). 8–12 concrete player-to-player interaction ideas for a horse jumping co-op game (2–4 players, phones/iPads), each with "how it plays" and griefing risk

### Takeaway
Twelve ideas below; each cites the precedent it borrows from. Griefing risk is rated Low / Medium / High and paired with the limiter that brings it down. The rule that governs all of them: nothing can cost a teammate more than ~3 seconds or anything persistent, and every prank must be visible as a *gag on the victim's screen* (sound + face), not just a penalty.

### Cited Findings
- Precedents used: collision feedback and rewarded bumping — [A Virus Named Tom](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/sometimes-frustrating-design-can-be-fun); grab/bump with instant respawn — [Fall Guys](https://www.gamerevolution.com/guides/655429-how-to-stop-fall-guys-griefers-from-grabbing-you); throwing to a teammate — [Overcooked 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overcooked_2); hug/hand-hold/taunt — [Gang Beasts](https://bit-tech.net/reviews/gaming/pc/interview-gang-beasts/1/); physical chain — [Chained Together](https://dailycampus.com/2024/11/12/the-backlog-ascend-with-friends-in-chained-together/); stacking saddles — [Goat Simulator 3](https://press-start.com.au/previews/2022/08/27/goat-simulator-3-hands-on-preview-an-even-wilder-sandbox/); point-stealing disruptors as a gamble — [Marked for Mayhem](https://www.martincid.com/technology-sv/gaming/marked-for-mayhem-first-public-playtest/); goose reaction animations and honk-triggered gags — [House House](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/behind-the-honk-an-i-untitled-goose-game-i-q-a), [ACMI](https://www.acmi.net.au/education/school-program-and-resources/honk-untitled-goose-exhibition-education-resource/); horse canon: spooking at nothing, flower-eating, rider polite to horse after fall, side-eye/nod — [Horse Nation](https://horsenation.com/?p=118712), [Horse Nation Devon](https://www.horsenation.com/?p=40388), [EssentiallySports](https://www.essentiallysports.com/us-sports-news-olympics-news-equestrian-news-despite-falling-off-her-horse-mid-training-rider-does-the-most-unpredictable-thing-in-a-viral-video/), [Daily Dot](https://dailydot.com/bjork-horse-of-absolute-agreement-meme), [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/lifestyle/horse-goes-viral-tiktok-funny-dance-moves-owner-twinkle-real-character); handicaps/assist modes — [Mario Party Jamboree](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/super-mario-party-jamboree), [Moving Out](https://www.well-played.com.au/made-in-australia-we-talk-moving-out-with-smg-studio/).

### Inferences (the ideas)
1. **Snort/Honk-spook.** Tap to make your horse snort; any teammate's horse within a few metres does a one-second telegraphed "ears back… SPOOK" sideways hop with a flailing rider, then carries on. *How it plays:* you ride up beside your sister before the water tray, snort, her pony teleports sideways two metres with a cartoon "!!" and a squeal; she loses ~1 second. *Griefing risk:* Medium. Limiters: disabled in the last three strides before a fence and mid-air; 20 s cooldown; three charges per round; the victim's horse gets a "spooked at absolutely nothing" caption and a tiny style bonus if she still clears the next fence (A Virus Named Tom's "victim is annoyed and funny").
2. **Carrot toss (horse eats the course).** Flick a carrot onto the ground; the next horse over it stops dead to munch for 1.5 s with a chewing face. *How it plays:* on the flat between fences, the big brother drops a carrot, the little sister's pony plants and chews, she mashes "go" while it ignores her. *Risk:* High if unconstrained. Limiters: only works on the flat (never within three strides of a fence); costs the thrower a stride too (shared cost, like a gamble); two carrots per rider per round; the eaten carrot adds +1 to the *team* "horse happiness" meter so the victim gains something.
3. **Flower-box snack.** Any horse ridden close past a decorated fence can grab the flowers (hold a button); you get a silly "+flowers" collectible and the fence loses its decoration for everyone, which makes it *less* spooky (easier) — a prank that helps the team. *How it plays:* "Pablo at Devon" moment; the judge NPC gasps. *Risk:* Low (purely cosmetic/positive).
4. **Bump-and-bounce with rewarded ricochet.** Horses collide physically; both ragdoll a little, the bumped horse pins its ears, the rider says "Oi!", and bouncing a teammate into a puddle/mud scores +5 to the *team* "chaos" bonus. *How it plays:* side-by-side gallop to the combination, shoulders touch, one of you lands in the water with a splash. *Risk:* Medium. Limiters: collisions never cause a fall by themselves (only a stumble); no bump damage mid-air; 2 s "no-bump" bubble right after a remount.
5. **Reins swap.** At a checkpoint flag, controls swap horses for 8 seconds (each player now steers the other's horse). *How it plays:* total mutual chaos, no victim; both horses may refuse because the "new rider" is wrong. *Risk:* Low (symmetric). Limiter: visible countdown; can be toggled off in lobby for younger players.
6. **Leg-up remount (rescue).** When a rider falls (cartoon bounce, hat flies off), a teammate who rides up within 3 s triggers a "leg-up" and the fallen rider remounts instantly; solo remount takes 4 s. *How it plays:* "I've got you!" — the rescuer gets a team style point; the fallen rider's horse, meanwhile, is eating grass. *Risk:* None; it's the counterweight to 1–4 (helping must always be worth more than pranking).
7. **Loose-horse catch.** A riderless horse trots off and, left alone, jumps the next fence by itself (beautifully — the "yeets itself" clip). Any teammate can catch it by riding alongside and holding; the catcher and the faller split a bonus. *How it plays:* the whole team laughs at the loose pony clearing an oxer riderless while its rider jogs after it. *Risk:* Low.
8. **Buddy-lead for refusals.** If a horse refuses (ears back, "nope" skid), a teammate can ride alongside and "lead" it over on the second attempt (the refusing horse follows the buddy). *How it plays:* the older sibling escorts the little one's nappy pony over the scary liverpool. *Risk:* None; it turns the classic refusal into a co-op verb instead of a punishment.
9. **Shared prank-token hazards.** Each round the team gets one token per player to drop a comic hazard on the course that affects *everyone, including the dropper*: a llama by fence 4, a flapping banner, a French bulldog, a "someone moved the mounting block". *How it plays:* players negotiate ("not the llama!"); the team score is shared so there is no victim. *Risk:* Low–Medium (one sibling can spam the scariest hazard). Limiter: tokens are per player and one per round; hazards are visible on the course map before the round starts.
10. **Rosette swipe on the victory lap.** After scores are banked, the winner's horse wears a rosette and the others can swipe it during a 15-second victory-lap chase, then it gets swiped back. *How it plays:* Steal a Brainrot energy with zero stakes — points are already locked. *Risk:* Low (nothing persists); make the swiped rosette immediately swipe-able back so it ends in a chase, not a loss.
11. **Horse & rider emote set (free, equal for all).** Side-eye, nod of absolute agreement, head-shake of disapproval, dramatic snort, spook-at-own-fart, lazy grass-munch; rider shrug, "I meant to do that", polite pat after a fall, crop drop. Emotes pause your horse briefly (so taunting costs time, like Gang Beasts). *Risk:* Low; no free chat, no paid emotes (the Stumble Guys fairness complaint).
12. **Blame-cam fail reel.** Every fall/refusal/spook is auto-clipped; at the round end a 10-second slow-mo reel plays with comedy sound and captions ("spooked at the empty corner", "ate the flowers"), with a one-tap "it was the pony's fault" button for whoever was blamed. *How it plays:* "hilarious in retrospect" is engineered rather than hoped for. *Risk:* Low; the button exists to defuse, not assign, blame.

### Gaps
- All twelve are my design proposals grounded in the cited precedents; none has been playtested. The cooldown/charge numbers are starting values, not evidence.

## Deliverable (c). Rules of thumb for keeping it funny and not mean between siblings aged 10–11

### Takeaway
Keep every prank cheap, visible, symmetric and reversible; make rescuing worth more than sabotaging; pool the score; add handicaps and an opt-out; replace chat with emotes; end every round with the fail reel. Each rule below is tied to the evidence that motivates it.

### Cited Findings
- Nobody actually gets hurt: "An important part of slapstick humour is that no one actually gets hurt" — [ACMI](https://www.acmi.net.au/education/school-program-and-resources/honk-untitled-goose-exhibition-education-resource/); "you can only fall, try again. You don't get hurt" — [Common Sense Media, HFF](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/human-fall-flat)
- Victim must get a funny reaction, not a silent penalty — [A Virus Named Tom](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/sometimes-frustrating-design-can-be-fun)
- Joyful, not mean — [West of Loathing, GDC 2018](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025010/There-s-Goofs-in-Them)
- Sabotage as a gamble, never a free griefing tool — [Marked for Mayhem](https://www.martincid.com/technology-sv/gaming/marked-for-mayhem-first-public-playtest/); cap per-player swing — [BGDF, snippet](https://bgdf.com/forum/game-creation/design-theory/oxymoronic-co-opcompetition-game)
- Pooled score "you're all in this together" — [Co-Optimus, snippet](https://co-optimus.com/editorial/976/page/2/indie-ana-co-op-and-the-dev-stories-you-re-all-in-this-together.html)
- Same tools for everyone (fairness complaints when punch/kick emotes are paid) — [Steam, Stumble Guys](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1677740/discussions/0/3488628040303483921/); "the root cause is rarely the tech itself—it's the perceived unfairness" — [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/when-siblings-fight-over-devices)
- Luck mitigates skill so the younger one still wins sometimes — [PS Blog, Fall Guys](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=221489); handicaps — [Mario Party Jamboree](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/super-mario-party-jamboree); Assist Mode — [Moving Out](https://www.well-played.com.au/made-in-australia-we-talk-moving-out-with-smg-studio/)
- Being *targeted* is the top kid complaint — [Common Sense Media, Fall Guys](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/fall-guys-ultimate-knockout); fast recovery is the accepted antidote — [GameRevolution](https://www.gamerevolution.com/guides/655429-how-to-stop-fall-guys-griefers-from-grabbing-you)
- Restrict the number of ways to fail to stop frustration — [Overcooked, Duncan](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-ghost-town-games-i-overcooked-i-)
- Keep conflict legible and simple: "explain what the users are doing within a sentence", "visually clean" levels — [PCGamesInsider](https://www.pcgamesinsider.biz/feature/69833/how-mediatonic-made-the-takeshis-castle-of-video-games-in-fall-guys/); "the conflict is always easy to read" — [Game Developer IGF, Goose](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-house-house-s-i-untitled-goose-game-i-)
- Pre-made emotes instead of chat — [Common Sense Media, Overcooked 2](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/game-reviews/overcooked-2)
- "Stressful in the moment, hilarious in retrospect"; "rage can make the experience funnier to look back on" — [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/media/pico-park-game), [Daily Campus](https://dailycampus.com/2023/11/14/the-backlog-destroy-your-friendships-with-pico-park/)
- Shorter sessions — Quinn: "Consider making shorter games" — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/exploring-what-it-takes-to-make-a-funny-game)
- Stealing real progress produces "kids crying on TikTok" — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/steal-a-brainrot-roblox-empire-review-popular-memes-1851787027)
- Hand-off ritual: the one finishing helps the next get set up — [Screenwise](https://screenwiseapp.com/guides/when-siblings-fight-over-devices)
- Test jokes early with new people — [Quinn, GDC 2015](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/exploring-what-it-takes-to-make-a-funny-game)

### Inferences (the rules)
1. **Three-second rule.** No prank may cost a teammate more than ~3 seconds, and nothing persistent (no lost horses, cosmetics, XP). The victory-lap rosette is the only "steal" allowed.
2. **The victim is the performer.** Every prank fires a sound, a face and a caption on the *victim's* screen; if the victim can't laugh at what they see, cut the prank.
3. **Serious-window lockout.** No horse-to-horse pranks in the last three strides before a fence or mid-air. Pranks live on the flat between fences, where failure is cheap.
4. **Sabotage costs the saboteur.** Every prank spends a stride, a charge or a token; the team's shared chaos/style bonus goes up either way, so there is never a pure loser.
5. **Rescue beats revenge.** The leg-up, loose-horse catch and buddy-lead always pay more team points than any prank; the game's economy should make "help" the dominant strategy for a 10-year-old.
6. **One pooled team score; individual "style" only as flair.** No end-of-round leaderboard between siblings by default (optional "who was silliest" award instead).
7. **Symmetric, free, identical tools.** Every emote and prank is available to every player from minute one; nothing bought, nothing unlocked by grind.
8. **Luck and handicaps.** Horses have a small random "mood" each round (one is lazy, one is spooky) so the better rider still fails sometimes; a per-player assist toggle (wider jump window, slower timers) that the other sibling can't see.
9. **Opt-out per prank type.** A lobby toggle lets a player switch off being spooked/carroted (but not collisions, which are the game); the toggle is private so no one is shamed for using it.
10. **Instant, funny recovery.** Falls are cartoon bounces with a flying hat; remount in ≤4 s solo, instantly with help; the horse never gets hurt or "ruined".
11. **Few ways to fail, all legible.** Keep the course readable in one sentence ("jump 8 fences, don't fall off") and telegraph every hazard a second before it fires so the laugh is anticipation, not ambush.
12. **No free chat, rich emotes.** Side-eye, nod, snort, pat, shrug — enough to taunt and to apologise.
13. **Mandatory fail reel.** Every round ends on a 10-second slow-mo of the funniest fails with a "pony's fault" button, so the last thing both siblings see is the shared laugh, not the score.
14. **Rounds of 2–3 minutes.** Short rounds limit how long a grudge can build and make "one more go" the natural response to a fall.
15. **Playtest with real sibling pairs early and often,** watching for the Pico Park failure mode ("purposely sabotages… likes to watch the world burn") — if a prank gets used only on the younger sibling, it needs a stronger cost or a lockout.

### Gaps
- These rules synthesise evidence from games for 7–14-year-olds; I found no study of sibling conflict specifically in co-op video games at ages 10–11, and no horse-game precedent to copy, so numbers (3 s, 3 strides, 2–3 min rounds) are starting hypotheses to validate in playtests.
