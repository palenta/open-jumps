## KQ1. Which control schemes get praised as "responsive/intuitive" and which get "clunky/unresponsive" — mobile horse games and mobile racers/runners/rhythm games?

### Takeaway
Across horse games and racers, the schemes that reviewers call intuitive are the *reduced* ones — auto-run/auto-steer with one or two big discrete inputs (sprint, jump, lane-swipe); the schemes that draw "clunky / difficult / imprecise" complaints are free virtual joysticks (Sky, Star Stable mobile), hold-to-accelerate-while-sliding-to-steer (Mario Kart Tour), and tilt. In horse games specifically, Rival Stars' own developer labels its left/right-steer + sprint button as "simple, intuitive controls that players are already familiar with" and its joystick "Equestrian Mode" as "a bit trickier to master", and the only jump-mechanic praise found is for a *timing bar whose size adapts* to the horse's state.

### Inferences
- The strongest pattern across all genres: reviewers praise schemes that *remove continuous analog steering* (auto-run + discrete taps/swipes) and criticise schemes that put a free joystick or continuous slide under a thumb. For horse games this maps directly onto Rival Stars' own split: "Normal" (steer-nudge + sprint) = familiar/intuitive; "Equestrian Mode" joystick = precision for experts.
- Complaints about horse-game *jumping* are about the timing window being opaque or too tight ("can't ever get the right jump time … jump cooldown"), while the praised jump mechanic (Rival Stars) exposes an on-screen error bar and makes the window size a visible, player-influenced variable (confidence/stamina, approach angle).
- "Less fun / boring" is the cost of over-reduction (TouchDrive, one-finger MKT). The sweet spot reviewers keep landing on is: two or three *meaningful* discrete decisions per fence (line, pace, take-off) with auto-locomotion underneath.
- Monetisation, crashes and grind dominate 1★ reviews of every horse game sampled far more than controls do; controls become a loud complaint only when a port is lazy (SSO mobile, Horse Riding Tales PC) or the scheme is a free joystick (Sky, SSO).

## KQ2. How do timing-based mobile games present the "hit the window" moment, and what window sizes feel fair (ms)?

### Takeaway
The readable presentations are (a) an approaching marker that must align with a fixed line/zone (Beatstar), (b) a bar with a visible "perfect" zone that *grows or shrinks* with the horse's state (Rival Stars show jumping), and (c) a start-gate meter with a green zone (Rival Stars, Wildshade hold-and-release). Published window numbers are from rhythm-game sources, not kids' games: tiered windows around ±45 / ±90 / ±135 ms in a JS implementation guide, and a patent proposing 0.4 s (easy) / 0.3 s / 0.2 s (hard) with latency offset; no child-specific ms data was found.

### Cited Findings
- Rival Stars show jumping: "Jump-Timing Indicator: Simple HUD cue shows jump quality; timing window adjusts with Jump stat, approach angle, and confidence"; "Perfect jumps and steady pace build confidence, widening the 'perfect' timing window" — [rivalstarshorseracing.com, Update 1.66 (Aug 2025)](https://www.rivalstarshorseracing.com/post/mobile-update-1-66-august-update)
- Player reaction to the shrinking bar: "I like that the 'error bar' to get a perfect jump decreases as the horse looses stamina, but I wish it changed size less rapidly at very low stamina" — [Steam thread, Nov 2025](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1166860/discussions/0/678482693017650607/)
- Rival Stars start gate: "Get the cursor in the green zone to come flying out" — [Pocket Gamer](https://www.pocketgamer.com/rival-stars-horse-racing/rival-stars-horse-racing-tips-to-help-you-flying-out-of-the-gate/); Wildshade perfect start = hold and release "just before the timer finishes ticking down" — [Equine on the Mind](https://equineonthemind.com/wildshade-fantasy-horse-race-game-review-tips-and-tricks-and-is-it-good-actually/)
- Beatstar: hit when "the glowing line on the block lines up with the line of the hit area" = Perfect+; still inside the coloured zone = Perfect; lowest tier only breaks combo; Perfect window described as lenient — [sspai, Sep 2021](https://sspai.com/post/68593)
- JS judgement-window guide: example tiers `sick ≤45 ms, good ≤90 ms, bad ≤135 ms`, else miss; "Using the absolute error makes early and late hits share the same grade, while retaining the signed error separately is useful for calibration feedback"; drive timing from the audio clock, not frame time — [dev.to, "Building Fair Rhythm-Game Judgement Windows in JavaScript"](https://dev.to/skywalker_2de7de5f97df567/building-fair-rhythm-game-judgement-windows-in-javascript-1og9)
- Patent (Harmonix-style rhythm input): window "may be adjusted based on difficulty, for example 0.4 s for easy, 0.3 s for medium, and 0.2 s for hard"; window may be shifted "forwards or backwards to account for latency based on the user's latency settings" — [US Patent 11,358,065](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11358065)
- osu! "offset processor" model: early-miss / early-valid / 0 ms / late-valid / late-miss regions; "miss windows play an important role in penalizing the spamming of hits"; "A valid window too large is also not desirable as that may cause desynchronization in note correlation; when hitting twice or more before or after a timing, the processor may start consistently processing hits one or more notes ahead or behind" — [osu! forum](https://osu.ppy.sh/community/forums/posts/7711842)
- "Latency calibration and precise timing are non-negotiable … the timing windows … need to be fair and readable: tight enough to demand skill but not so punishing that they feel impossible, and clearly communicated" — [Bugnet, How to Design a Rhythm Game](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-design-a-rhythm-game)
- One-tap runner guidance: "coyote time so a late tap still clears the gap … In a one-tap game the input is almost the whole game" — [Summer Engine](https://www.summerengine.com/ai-endless-runner-maker.md)
- Kids' input error base rate: in a study of 43 touch targets, children's touches landed outside the target 46% of the time vs 32% for adults, implying children need more tolerance in how touch maps to targets — [Brown & Anthony, CHI 2012 EIST](https://lisa-anthony.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/brown-and-anthony-chi2012eist.pdf)
- Rhythm Heaven's often-quoted ±25–60 ms per-level windows appear only on a page of questionable provenance — [unverified](https://phdns.latahcountyid.gov/article/rhythm-heaven-seasawiki-master-the-beat)

### Inferences
- For a 10–11-year-old on a phone browser (extra input + audio latency, no native vibration), a jump window should start far wider than rhythm-game "perfect" (±45 ms) — the patent's *easy* 400 ms total window and Beatstar's "lenient Perfect + tight Perfect+" layering are the better models: make the *coarse* tier (clear the fence, no fault) ≈ ±150–200 ms and reserve a ±50–80 ms inner tier for style points. These ms values are design inferences, not measured child data.
- Make the window a *visible* object that the player can widen through good riding (steady pace, straight approach) — Rival Stars' Confidence Meter was adopted after a year of player feedback and the shrinking "error bar" was the one jump feature a player singled out for praise.
- Keep a miss zone (osu!) so spamming the jump button on approach is penalised as a refusal/early take-off; otherwise a one-tap scheme degenerates into button mashing.
- Grade by absolute error but *show* signed error ("early"/"late" arrow) — directly useful for kids who ride (maps to "chipped in" vs "stood off").

### Gaps
- No ms numbers found for Beatstar, Rival Stars, Subway Surfers, or any kids' game; no GDC talk with child timing data was retrievable.
- Celeste/TowerFall coyote-time and jump-buffer frame counts (Maddy Thorson's write-up) were fetched but the numeric passage was not extracted before the search quota closed — [source page](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_towerfall_physics/index.html).

## KQ3. What do UX research and platform guidance say about touch targets, thumb zones and landscape layouts for children 8–12?

### Takeaway
Guidance converges on: ≥1 cm × 1 cm (adults, NN/g), ≥2 cm × 2 cm for younger children (NN/g), 44 × 44 pt (Apple), 24 × 24 CSS px legal minimum (WCAG 2.2 AA); children miss targets markedly more than adults (46% vs 32%); 9–12-year-olds are a distinct group from 6–8 and resent being designed for as "babies"; most phone users touch with one thumb and prefer the centre of the screen; Apple's game-controls HIG explicitly publishes an "ideal placement" heat map for touch controls on an iPhone in landscape.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g: "you must distinguish between young (3–5), mid-range (6–8), and older (9–12) children"; "children as young as 3 can use websites and apps"; "The main predictor of children's ability to use websites is their amount of prior experience"; kids reacted negatively to content "even one school grade below or above their own level" — [NN/g, Children's UX (Sherwin & Nielsen)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/)
- NN/g physical development: "Touchscreen designs for kids under 9 should emphasize swiping, tapping, and dragging"; "We recommend at least 2cm × 2cm touch targets for young children (4 times bigger than the 1cm x 1cm recommended target size for adult users)"; a 5 mm close button defeated two 7-year-olds — [NN/g, Design for Kids Based on Their Stage of Physical Development (Feifei Liu)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/)
- NN/g touch targets: "Interactive elements must be at least 1cm × 1cm (0.4in × 0.4in) to support adequate selection time and prevent fat-finger errors"; "if an app … is to be used when the user is moving, targets … should be bigger"; crowding causes slips, so spacing matters as much as size — [NN/g, Touch Targets on Touchscreens (May 2019)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)
- NN/g cognition: "Use existing mental models and knowledge about the world"; every child tested reached for the eraser instead of undo/redo; kids under 6 missed subtle character-face feedback — [NN/g, Designing for Kids: Cognitive Considerations](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/kids-cognition/)
- Academic: children's touches fell outside the target 46% of the time vs 32% for adults (Brown & Anthony) — [CHI 2012 EIST paper](https://lisa-anthony.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/brown-and-anthony-chi2012eist.pdf); children "miss onscreen touch targets far more often than adults, especially for smaller targets" — [Anthony et al., JPUC 2013](https://lisa-anthony.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/anthony-et-al-jpuc2013.pdf); 54 children aged 5–10: Fitts' law with nominal target width fits children's finger input (R² = 0.93) better than finger-adjusted FFitts — [Woodward et al., AVI 2020](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2021/05/woodward-et-al-AVI2020.pdf); on tablets "children responded faster and slipped less with touch than pen" — [Woodward et al., CHI 2016 slides](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2017/05/Woodward-et-al-CHI2016-presentation-slides.pdf)
- WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 (AA): targets smaller than 24 × 24 CSS px are "undersized" and must have a 24 px spacing circle; "It can be beneficial to provide an option to increase the active target area without increasing the visible target size" — [W3C Understanding 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)
- Apple HIG, Designing for games: "Buttons that are too small or too close together can frustrate players and make gameplay less fun. Each platform defines a recommended minimum …" and text must meet each platform's minimum size — [Apple HIG, Designing for games](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/designing-for-games); Apple HIG, Game controls: supports platform default input even when controllers exist because "not every player might have access to one"; includes a graphic of "ideal placement for touch controls for an iPhone in landscape orientation" (touch-input heat map) — [Apple HIG, Game controls](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/game-controls); the HIG accessibility page's 44 × 44 pt minimum hit-target rule is the figure every iOS reference quotes (page is JS-rendered; the sentence was not extracted in this run) — [Apple HIG, Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)
- Thumb research: "75% of users touch the screen only with one thumb"; "People hold phones in multiple ways … They change their methods of grasping their phone without realizing it"; "They prefer to view and touch the center of the screen" — [Hoober, Design for Fingers, Touch, and People (2017)](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2017/03/design-for-fingers-touch-and-people-part-1.php); 36% of observed users "cradle" (two hands hold, one thumb/finger touches; 72% thumb) — [Hoober, How Do Users Really Hold Mobile Devices? (2013)](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)
- Google Play Families policy covers target-audience declaration, ads and data handling; no touch-target or control-layout guidance was found in it — [Google Play Families Policies](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)
- Kids' racing precedent: "The tilt controls are easy to use, and the game's margin of error in steering is very forgiving … acceleration isn't in the control of the player" — [Common Sense Media, Disney Super Speedway](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/disney-super-speedway)

### Inferences
- For 10–11-year-olds, use adult minimums as a floor and the 2 cm rule for anything timing-critical (jump button): a timed tap under stress behaves like "use while moving" in NN/g terms. On an iPhone that is roughly 75–80 pt; on iPad use the same physical size, not the same point size.
- Because 75% of users touch with one thumb and prefer the centre, a phone layout should let the *primary* timing input be "tap anywhere on the right half / anywhere on screen", not a small button — the same decision that made Alto and one-finger Mario Kart work.
- Treat 10–11s as "older children": they will reject anything that looks pitched at 6–8 (NN/g), so assist modes must be framed as *rider* settings ("green horse / schoolmaster", "auto-distance on") rather than "easy mode for kids".
- WCAG's "enlarge the active area without enlarging the visual" is the right tool for the jump input: a modest visible button with a much larger hit region, plus input tolerance (coyote-time-style) to absorb the ~46% child miss rate.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures 8–12-year-olds' *timed* tap accuracy (ms), only spatial accuracy; the Anthony/Woodward corpus is mostly ages 5–10.
- Apple's landscape touch heat-map image could not be inspected (only its alt text); exact zones unknown.
- No NN/g or academic source specifically on *landscape two-thumb* layouts for children was found.

## KQ4. How did Mario & Sonic Olympics, Wii/Switch horse games and Rival Stars translate "riding" into simple inputs, and were those praised or mocked?

### Takeaway
Every console translation reduces riding to: an impulse for "go/faster" (swing the remote, hold up, sprint button), a single jump button pressed on timing, and a gentle left/right lead to stay on the "ideal line"; the Switch/3DS Mario & Sonic entries additionally score staying on the line and not knocking fences. Rival Stars kept exactly that trio for a year, then added fixed pace "gears", a confidence-driven window and hard-turn — and its only motion-control version (VR reins) was the one reviewers called frustrating.

### Inferences
- The lowest-common-denominator riding grammar that survived three console generations is **impulse-to-go, line-nudge, timed jump** — none of them continuous analog. That grammar is also what Rival Stars calls "controls players are already familiar with", so a tween who has played any of these will recognise it instantly.
- What got *mocked* was either waggle for speed (replaced by "more accurate Joy-Con controls" by 2019) or literal reins in VR; what got *praised* was auto-locomotion plus a visible jump-timing cue.
- Rival Stars' 2025 additions (fixed gears, confidence meter, hard turn) are the closest published analogue to real riding concepts (collected vs extended canter, rhythm, straightness) implemented on a phone, and they came from a year of player feedback — a strong signal for a rider-audience game.

### Gaps
- Tokyo 2020's equestrian event control page returned 404; exact Switch button/motion mapping unverified.
- Wii "My Horse & Me" reviews (Blogcritics, CheatCC, NWR) were not fetchable (403/502); whether its waggle riding was mocked is unconfirmed.
- Pure Nintendo's 2026 review covers racing but did not describe the show-jumping inputs on Joy-Con.

## KQ5. Tilt steering on iPad; haptics in iOS Safari; browser limits on orientation/fullscreen/gyroscope (state as of 2026)

### Takeaway
Tilt is "realistic when well tuned" but reviewers call it fatiguing on iPad and imprecise on winding tracks, and Pocket Gamer flatly says it has "never been good"; in a browser it additionally costs an iOS permission prompt that users cannot later revoke from Settings. Haptics: Safari on iOS still does not implement the Vibration API (through Safari 27.2 per caniuse, Nov 2026); the only working trick is programmatically clicking a hidden `<input type="checkbox" switch>` (Safari 17.4+). Orientation lock is unsupported in Safari (any version), element fullscreen is only "partial" on iOS, so a landscape lock must be done with CSS rotation or by prompting the user, and a Home-Screen web app (any site can be one since Safari 26) is the closest thing to a locked, chrome-less canvas.

### Cited Findings
**Tilt**
- "tilt controls have never been good. There's no racing game on the planet which has benefitted from having you awkwardly swing your hands about" — [Pocket Gamer, 2018](https://www.pocketgamer.com/asphalt-9-legends/asphalt-9s-stripped-back-controls-are-the-best-way-to-play-the-game-heres-five-r/)
- iPad racing accessory piece: tilting an iPad is fun at first "but the arm strain and sweaty palms wear it down after a few minutes" — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/this-ipad-racing-gadget-is-crazier-than-the-mario-kart-wii-wheel/); PadRacer: iPad "too large to tilt far and fast enough" — [Engadget, 2011](https://www.engadget.com/2011-05-25-padracer-lets-you-draw-your-own-track-join-two-ipads-together-a.html)
- Forum consensus (2012): tilt "realistic but prone to sudden jolts … poor fit for winding tracks" — [TouchArcade forum](https://toucharcade.com/community/threads/which-is-the-better-tilt-or-touch.174902/); tilt + touch combo "aggravating" — [Pocket Gamer, Drift Mania](https://www.pocketgamer.com/drift-mania-championship/review)
- Positive when tuned: NFS Shift "tilting the phone feels natural" — [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/202295/needforspeedshift.html); Riptide GP tilt "worked better than expected" — [AppSpy](https://www.appspy.com/review/5013/riptide-gp); for younger kids "tilt controls are easy to use, and the game's margin of error in steering is very forgiving" — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/disney-super-speedway)
- 2018 study found no statistically significant difference between tilt and touch steering results, but n = 1 player × 15 races — [ResearchGate (PDF)](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Muhammad-Suhaib-6/publication/328127724_Tilt_or_Touch_An_Evaluation_of_Steering_Control_of_Racing_Game_on_Tablet_or_Smartphone/links/5bd95e1492851c6b279b9df9/Tilt-or-Touch-An-Evaluation-of-Steering-Control-of-Racing-Game-on-Tablet-or-Smartphone.pdf)
- Browser gyro: `DeviceOrientationEvent.requestPermission()` "requires transient activation, meaning that it must be triggered by a UI event such as a button click" — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/DeviceOrientationEvent/requestPermission_static); supported in Safari on iOS 14.5+ (not on desktop Safari) — [caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_deviceorientationevent_requestpermission_static); "on the Safari iOS side, there is no UI for controlling the permission" so users cannot revoke it from Settings — [W3C device-apis log, May 2024](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-device-apis-log/2024May/0063.html)

**Haptics in Safari**
- Vibration API, Safari on iOS: "Not supported" for every version 3.2 → 27.2; Chrome supported since 30 — [caniuse (Nov 2026)](https://caniuse.com/vibration); MDN lists `navigator.vibrate()` as "Limited availability … does not work in some of the most widely-used browsers" — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Navigator/vibrate)
- Workaround: WebHaptics creates a hidden `<input type="checkbox" switch>` (the `switch` attribute is Safari-only) and clicks its label programmatically, which fires iOS's built-in switch haptic; it uses `navigator.vibrate()` where available and the switch trick otherwise — [azukiazusa.dev (2025)](https://azukiazusa.dev/en/blog/ios-safari-web-haptics); the switch control was introduced in Safari 17.4 — [iOS Haptics Tester](https://rapidtoolset.com/en/tool/ios-haptics-tester); polyfill that "works inside onClick, onPointerMove and onInput" — [ios-vibrator-pro-max (GitHub)](https://github.com/samdenty/ios-vibrator-pro-max)
- On some iOS builds `navigator.vibrate` exists as a function but "always returns false without triggering haptics", so feature-detect by behaviour not `typeof` — [Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/fix-web-game-vibrate-api-not-available-ios-safari); "as of 2026, Apple has not indicated plans to support the W3C Vibration API in Safari" — [web-haptics docs](https://www.mintlify.com/lochie/web-haptics/advanced/browser-support)
- Native reference: "components like switches, sliders, and pickers automatically play haptic feedback on supported iPhone models" (the mechanism the web trick piggybacks on) — [Apple HIG, Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)

**Orientation lock / fullscreen**
- `ScreenOrientation.lock()`: Safari "Not supported" 3.1 → 27.2 — [caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_screenorientation_lock); MDN marks it "Limited availability" — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/ScreenOrientation/lock)
- WebKit bug 257695 (filed Jun 2023, resolved WORKSFORME): lock depends on the HTML Fullscreen API, which iPhone Safari lacks for arbitrary elements; "it may work as a Home Screen web application"; on iPad "multitasking apps cannot lock their orientation"; the Screen Orientation locking flag is disabled by default (2024) — [WebKit Bugzilla](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=257695)
- Fullscreen API: Safari on iOS "Partial support" 12 → 27.2 (desktop-class element fullscreen is iPad-only; iPhone fullscreen is video-only) — [caniuse](https://caniuse.com/fullscreen); MDN "Limited availability" — [MDN requestFullscreen](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Element/requestFullscreen)
- Safari 26 (Sept 2025): "Every site can be a web app on iOS and iPadOS" — any site added to the Home Screen can now open as a standalone web app (previously required `apple-mobile-web-app-capable` meta or a manifest `display` value) — [WebKit blog](https://webkit.org/blog/17333/webkit-features-in-safari-26-0/)

### Inferences
- Tilt should be, at most, an opt-in *assist* for line nudging on phones, never the default and never on iPad: it adds a permission dialog on first use (not re-promptable if declined), can't be revoked by parents from Settings, fatigues iPad users within minutes, and reviewers across 15 years rate it below touch for precision.
- Haptics in the browser: plan for *no* haptics on iPhone Safari except a single-pulse "tick" via the switch trick, which must be triggered inside a user-gesture handler — good enough for "take-off confirmed" or "rail down", not for a stride-rhythm pulse. Android Chrome can use `navigator.vibrate()` patterns. Audio + screen flash must carry the rhythm cue.
- Landscape: implement a CSS "please rotate" overlay + `matchMedia('(orientation: portrait)')` and design the HUD to survive both orientations; encourage Add-to-Home-Screen (now one tap on Safari 26) for a chrome-less canvas and to avoid Safari's toolbar eating the thumb zone.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 iPad-specific tilt-steering reviews found (sources are 2010–2018); whether modern iPad Pro/Air tilt is less fatiguing is unverified.
- Whether iOS 26 Home-Screen web apps honour `screen.orientation.lock()` in practice was not test-verified; WebKit's comment says "may".

## KQ6. Synthesis — (a) comparison table, (b) recommended shortlist for a tween show-jumping browser game, (c) cue/feedback designs players found most readable

### Takeaway
Recommend auto-locomotion with three discrete rider decisions per fence (line nudge, pace gear, timed take-off) delivered as a one-thumb "tap-anywhere jump + edge swipes" layout on phones and a mirrored two-thumb layout on iPad; a rhythm layer (tap-per-stride) should be an optional confidence-building bonus, not a gate, because browser latency and the lack of iOS haptics make strict rhythm judgement unfair. Virtual joystick and tilt go in as expert/assist toggles only.

### Inferences

**(a) Comparison table of schemes**

**(b) Recommended shortlist (2–3 schemes)**

1. **"Three decisions, auto-canter" — phone, one thumb (primary).** Horse auto-follows the course line at the current gear. Player: (i) *line* — swipe left/right anywhere (or tap left/right screen edge) to nudge the track by one "lane" toward the fence, (ii) *pace* — tap a 2 cm gear button (Collected ↔ Extended; Gallop only on the gallop stretch), (iii) *take-off* — tap anywhere in the right 60% of the screen when the approaching-fence bar's marker enters the zone. Window = coarse "clear" tier (~±150–200 ms, inferred) + inner "perfect" tier, widened by steady pace and straight approach (RSHR confidence model). This is literally Rival Stars "Normal Controls + Refusals + gears", which has the only positively reviewed mobile jump timing found, delivered with Alto/MKT-style one-finger ergonomics.
2. **Same grammar, two thumbs — iPad landscape.** Left thumb: two large line-nudge zones (or a short swipe strip) in the lower-left; right thumb: gear button plus a tap-anywhere jump region on the right half. Mirrors the console trio (go / lead left-right / jump) that survived from Wii 2012 to Switch 2026, avoids joystick occlusion complaints, keeps targets ≥ 2 cm and away from the Safari toolbar.
3. **Rhythm-assisted approach (optional layer on 1 or 2).** A stride meter pulses on the canter beat for the last 4–5 strides; tapping (or just holding the gear) on beat builds "confidence", which widens the take-off window; off-beat taps do nothing (no penalty), so latency never fails a kid. Judge tiers Beatstar-style (lenient Perfect, tight Perfect+). Audio + flash carry the beat; a single switch-trick haptic "tick" on iPhone confirms take-off only. Validate with real 10–11-year-old riders before making it score-bearing.

Do **not** ship as default: free virtual joystick, tilt, hold-to-accelerate-while-sliding. Offer "Equestrian mode" (joystick) and "tilt line assist" as explicit expert/assist toggles the way PikPok did.

**(c) Cue/feedback designs players found most readable**
- *Approaching-fence timing bar with a visible, state-driven perfect zone* (RSHR "error bar" that shrinks/widens with stamina→confidence; the one praised jump cue). Change its size smoothly — the player complaint was that it "changed size too rapidly" at low stamina.
- *Marker-meets-line hit judgement with tiered feedback where the lowest tier only breaks combo* (Beatstar). Shows early/late direction, not just pass/fail.
- *Start-gate meter with a green zone* (RSHR) and *hold-and-release-before-the-timer-ends* (Wildshade) — both learned instantly by players, but Wildshade's was undocumented; put it in the tutorial.
- *Ideal-line guide on the ground* (Mario & Sonic 3DS "stay on the ideal line") so the line decision is readable before the pace decision.
- *Post-jump HUD quality cue* ("Simple HUD cue shows jump quality", RSHR 1.66) plus confidence meter visibly rising — immediate, kid-legible reward.
- *Big, centred, spaced targets* (NN/g 2 cm for timing-critical buttons; WCAG "enlarge active area without enlarging visual"); avoid thumbs covering the fence (Macworld's "thumbs smother the scenery" — keep take-off tap region off the fence itself).

### Gaps
- No published mobile horse game was found that uses tap-per-stride rhythm, so scheme 3 has no direct review evidence; nearest evidence is Beatstar's reception and RSHR's confidence meter.
- The ms window sizes proposed are inferences from rhythm-game sources and child spatial-error rates; no child *temporal* accuracy data was located.
- Reddit design-community threads and GDC talks on mobile racing/riding controls could not be collected because the session's web-search budget was exhausted; a follow-up pass could add them.
